# Internal transcript — ask-the-council run

**User message:** "I have two job offers on the table. Job A pays about 15% more but has a brutal commute and unpredictable hours. Job B pays less but is fully remote with a much saner schedule. Which one should I take?"

---

## Step 1 — Intake

Checked the four intake items against context:

1. **Reversibility.** Not stated, but inferable: a job choice is not a one-step undo. Backing out of an accepted offer or quitting a new job within months costs reputation, burns a reference, and (for Job A specifically) means eating the brutal-commute period before the exit. Two-way door, but an expensive one to walk back through.
2. **The real constraint.** Not stated. Could be money (needs the 15%), time/energy (commute + unpredictability), or someone else's schedule (a partner/dependents). No explicit signal either way — treated as unknown and folded into the panel's questions rather than assumed.
3. **Deadline.** Not stated. No evidence of urgency mentioned (no "offer expires Friday"), so treated as self-paced rather than forcing a rushed read.
4. **What "good" looks like in six months.** Not stated. Inferred candidate framings (rested and stable vs. richer but stretched) were left as an open question for the Framer to raise rather than assumed on the user's behalf.

Per the skill, up to 3 clarifying questions are allowed before proceeding, but the intake gaps here (constraint, deadline, six-month picture) don't block reasoning — they become explicit unknowns the panel flags and the Chair's "what would flip this" line addresses, rather than a hard stop. Proceeded to routing.

### Routing test (applied explicitly)

1. **Can it be undone in one step, cheaply, today, if it's wrong?** No. Accepting either offer means resigning from/declining the other, likely giving notice somewhere, and — for Job A — living the commute/hours reality for some real stretch before any exit is credible. Not a cheap, low-friction reversal.
2. **Is the downside bounded and small?** No. This is income, daily schedule, health, and (potentially) household logistics for however long the job lasts — not hours or minor money.
3. **Would a full seat have something load-bearing and distinct to say?** Yes, clearly: the Accountant's real-cost math (commute costs, stress-driven spending) plausibly changes which number is actually bigger; the Stakeholder's read on who else absorbs "unpredictable hours" plausibly changes the answer if there's a partner/dependents; the Long View's growth-trajectory question is a real, distinct axis the other seats don't cover.

All three gate answers are "no" / "yes-to-seat-relevance" — none clear the quick-mode bar. Routes to full panel.

**Mode line decided:** "Mode: full panel — a job choice with a real pay gap, a brutal commute, and unpredictable hours isn't cheap to undo and isn't a small-stakes call, and the real-cost math and the 'who else absorbs this' read both plausibly change the answer."

---

## Step 2 — Panel convened

**Specialist seat:** Not seated. Nothing here hinges on a domain fact outside the panel's reasoning (no legal, medical, tax, or hard technical constraint at stake) — this is a values/tradeoff decision, and adding a fake "career coach" specialist would produce false authority rather than real information. Noted explicitly rather than silently skipped.

### The Framer
"The real question isn't which paycheck is bigger — it's what a 'brutal commute' and 'unpredictable hours' actually cost you that a 15% raise doesn't buy back: sleep, evenings, patience, the people waiting for you at home. Notice you already named B 'saner' — some part of you has scored this already, and you might be here for permission more than a decision. Before picking a side, ask what 'good' looks like in six months: rested and present, or richer and running on fumes?"

### The Builder
"This isn't a $20 gadget — but you can still de-risk it cheaply before signing anything: drive Job A's actual commute at the real time you'd leave and come home, once, before deciding on vibes alone. Ask each company one direct question — Job A: 'what does a bad week of unpredictable hours actually look like, hour-wise?'; Job B: 'is the salary band fixed or is there room to negotiate?' Real answers from both beat guessing from the job description."

### The Skeptic
"Job A fails the way this kind of offer tends to fail: the commute turns out worse than the estimate, 'unpredictable hours' turns out to mean nights and weekends on short notice, and eighteen months in you're burnt out and job-hunting again — except now you've also spent the goodwill of leaving a job that fast. Job B fails quieter: the lower pay stops feeling fine the first time a real expense hits, and 'fully remote' turns into invisible-to-leadership, so raises and attention go to whoever's in the room. Both have a specific, plausible failure mode — the question is which one you can actually see coming and manage, not which one sounds worse on paper."

### The Accountant
"Fifteen percent sounds like the number that matters, but it isn't the real number until you subtract what the commute costs — gas or fare, car wear, the hours themselves at whatever your time is worth, plus the takeout and convenience spending that shows up when you're too wrecked to cook. Unpredictable hours add a cost that never shows up on a payslip either: you can't reliably plan anything else in your life around that schedule, and that has its own price. Do that subtraction with your real numbers before assuming Job A's raise is worth more than Job B's stability — for a lot of people in exactly this setup it isn't, but you won't know until you run it with your own figures."

### The Long View
"Money and hours aren't the only thing that compounds — visibility does too, and if Job A has a real growth track with people who can promote you, that can be worth more over five years than either the raise or the commute pain, while a remote job with no one championing you can quietly cap your ceiling. But if Job A's schedule chaos is the kind that erodes health, relationships, or the standing to even stay long enough to benefit from that growth, none of the five-year upside gets collected. The question this seat actually needs answered: is Job A's harder path a means to something bigger, or just harder?"

### The Stakeholder
"Unpredictable hours don't stay contained to you — whoever shares your evenings, your commute-adjacent responsibilities (a partner, kids, a pet, an aging parent) absorbs the unpredictability as much as you do, and they didn't get a vote on the offer. If there's someone in that position, ask them directly what they'd actually do if Job A's hours blow up a plan for the third time in a month — not what they'd tolerate in theory. If you live alone and answer only to yourself, this seat has much less to say here."

## Step 3 — Consensus check

Not unanimous, so the forced "strongest case against" escalation does not trigger — but the disagreement is stated explicitly rather than assumed:

- **Skeptic** and **Accountant** lean toward B under most plausible numbers, but conditionally (Accountant explicitly says "for a lot of people... it isn't [worth more]," not "it never is").
- **Stakeholder** leans toward B only if someone else's schedule is on the line; explicitly neutral if the user lives alone.
- **Long View** is the genuine outlier — it opens a real path where Job A is correct (growth/visibility compounding), which is the opposite lean from Skeptic/Accountant/Stakeholder.
- **Framer** and **Builder** don't land on a side at all — they reframe and propose cheap information-gathering instead.

This is real, structural disagreement (not surface-level hedging), so Step 3's "unanimous panel = red flag" trigger does not apply here. No manufactured objection was needed because the Long View seat already supplies the genuine counter-case. This was checked deliberately, not skipped by default.

## Step 5 — Chair synthesis

Chair's read of the panel: three seats (Skeptic, Accountant, Stakeholder) lean toward Job B under most plausible real-world numbers, but all three attach real conditions rather than a flat verdict. One seat (Long View) opens a genuine, undefeated case for Job A contingent on a growth track that hasn't been confirmed. Two seats (Framer, Builder) don't take a side at all — they push toward cheap fact-finding before either camp's case can be trusted.

Given that the user supplied no evidence of a confirmed growth track at Job A, no stated financial goal requiring the 15%, and no denial of having a partner/dependents, the Chair weighted the panel toward the default that doesn't gamble health/relationships against an unconfirmed upside — Job B — while explicitly making that default conditional and cheap to overturn: a same-week conversation with Job A about growth and hours, per the Builder's and Long View's questions, is what would flip it. This is why the Decision line is phrased as a conditional imperative rather than a flat pick, and why Confidence is medium rather than high — the unknowns (real dollar gap, actual advancement path, who else is affected) are large enough that a different set of facts plausibly changes the Decision, not just the Confidence.

The "Strongest objection" slot in the template was given to the Long View's case for Job A, since it's the one genuine, non-resolved counter-argument the panel produced (as opposed to the Skeptic's failure-mode framing, which cuts against both options rather than for one). "Why it doesn't [lose outright]" reflects that this is a genuinely close call per the panel's own disagreement, not a manufactured concession — the template's alternate phrasing for close decisions was used deliberately rather than defaulting to "why it loses."
