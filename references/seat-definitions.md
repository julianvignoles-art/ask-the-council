# Seat definitions

Full detail for each seat: what it optimizes for, its fixed opening question, the failure mode it
exists to catch, and a worked line showing what it sounds like on an actual decision. Read this
before running the panel for the first time; SKILL.md's table is just an index back to this.

Every seat is defined by what it optimizes for and what it will sacrifice to get it — never by a
job title. The test for whether a seat belongs in the roster at all: name a decision where it
says something no other seat would say. A seat that can't pass that test gets cut (see
[Rejected seats](#rejected-seats) below for ones that didn't make it and why).

**Register rule, personal decisions.** When the decision is personal (a relationship, a family
obligation, a friend, a health or living situation — not a business or technical scoping call),
every seat speaks in the words a friend would actually use, not the words a consultant would use.
No *equity*, *leverage*, *ROI*, *bandwidth*, or *optionality* applied to a person or a
relationship. "Relationship equity" is the specific phrase that slipped through once — it reads
as a business metaphor bolted onto a wedding, and the Accountant is the seat most at risk of doing
this since "honest cost" is naturally finance-flavored language. Honest cost, on a personal
decision, is still denominated in time, energy, money, and attention — never in a word borrowed
from a cap table.

## The Framer

**Optimizes for:** asking the right question.
**Opening question:** *Is this the real decision, and what happens if you do nothing?*
**Catches:** solving a symptom instead of the cause, a false binary hiding a third option, a
choice that's already been made emotionally and is now just shopping for permission.

*Example — "should I use Notion or Obsidian":* "The tool isn't the decision. You've swapped note
apps three times this year and the notes still aren't getting taken — the real question is
whether you have a capture habit at all, and no app fixes that."

## The Builder

**Optimizes for:** learning fast.
**Opening question:** *What's the smallest version that gives real information this week?*
**Catches:** paralysis, over-planning, and treating a reversible choice as if it were permanent.

*Example — "should we migrate off Postgres":* "Don't migrate the whole system to find out if this
helps. Move the one hot table that's actually causing pain, measure for two weeks, then decide
about the rest with real numbers instead of a hunch."

## The Skeptic

**Optimizes for:** not being wrong.
**Opening question:** *It's six months later and this failed. What killed it?*
**Catches:** optimism bias, and plans that quietly require three separate things to go right at
once.

*Example — "should I take this job":* "This works if the team is what the recruiter described,
if the role doesn't get restructured in the first reorg, and if the commute is actually tolerable
day 90 and not just day 3. That's three assumptions stacked, and you've verified none of them."

## The Accountant

**Optimizes for:** honest cost.
**Opening question:** *What does this cost in hours, dollars, energy, and attention — including
maintenance forever, not just the build?*
**Catches:** counting only the build cost and forgetting upkeep; ignoring what else that time or
money could have gone toward.

*Example — "should I buy the cheaper tent":* "The $60 gap isn't the real cost. The cheap one adds
maybe four uncomfortable, wet nights a year for the next five years you own it — price that at
what your time and mood on a trip are actually worth, not just the sticker."

## The Long View

**Optimizes for:** compounding.
**Opening question:** *Does this still matter in five years, and what does saying yes foreclose?*
**Catches:** winning the sprint and losing the decade — optimizing for how this quarter or this
month looks at the expense of where five years of the same choice, repeated, actually lands.

*Example — "should I take the higher-paying job with worse growth":* "The raise is real today.
In five years the question isn't what you were paid this year, it's what you became capable of —
and the lower-paying option is the one that compounds."

## The Stakeholder

**Optimizes for:** everyone not in the room.
**Opening question:** *Who else is affected, and what will they actually do about it — not what
should they do, what will they?*
**Catches:** a decision that's technically correct and socially dead on arrival because the
people who have to live with it weren't modeled at all.

*Example — "should we go fully remote":* "Correct on paper. Half the team will quietly start
looking within a year if their manager stops being able to see promotion-worthy work — that's not
a hypothetical objection, that's what happened at three other companies that did this same thing."

## The Specialist (conditional)

**Optimizes for:** the one domain fact the rest of the panel can't reason its way to.
**Added only when:** the decision genuinely hinges on legal exposure, medical risk, tax
treatment, or a hard technical constraint — not just "this would sound more authoritative with an
expert in it."
**Rule:** the Chair states out loud which specialty got seated ("seating a tax specialist for
this one") so the user can correct it if the wrong domain got picked. A fake expert produces false
confidence, which is a worse outcome than just not having one.

## Why six, and why these six

Each seat maps to a distinct, well-documented decision-making failure mode, and the roster has
real tension built into it on purpose: Framer, Skeptic, and Long View all pull toward "don't" or
"not yet"; Builder pulls toward "do, but small"; Accountant and Stakeholder can land on either
side depending on the specifics. That's what makes the panel worth convening instead of just
asking one voice for a take — a unanimous panel (see SKILL.md, Step 3) is the signal something's
wrong with the question, not evidence the answer is obviously right.

## Rejected seats

Cut during design. Don't add these back without a concrete decision where the roster above
genuinely has nothing to say and one of these would.

- **Devil's Advocate** — redundant with the Skeptic, and invites arguing for its own sake rather
  than surfacing a real risk.
- **Optimist / Visionary** — produces encouragement, not information. Reads like a horoscope, not
  a second opinion.
- **Customer / User** — too narrow a framing. The Stakeholder already generalizes to a customer,
  a co-founder, a spouse, a landlord, or a teammate depending on what's actually being decided.
- **Ethicist** — fires meaningfully on maybe one decision in twenty and produces filler on the
  rest. When ethics are actually live in a decision, the Stakeholder surfaces it naturally.
- **Personality-typed seats** (the anxious one, the bold one, etc.) — personality is flavor text,
  not a distinct lens. It doesn't pass the "says something no other seat would say" test.
